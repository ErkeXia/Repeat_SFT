- Thus, the graph must be **free of both 3-cycles and 4-cycles**.

This means we are looking for the **maximum number of edges** in a **graph with 10 vertices**, **no 3-cycles**, and **no 4-cycles** — in other words, a graph with **girth at least 5**.

### Known Graph: Petersen Graph

The **Petersen graph** is a well-known graph in graph theory with the following properties:

- **10 vertices**
- **15 edges**
- **3-regular** (each vertex has degree 3)
- **Girth 5** (no cycles of length 3 or 4)
- **Triangle-free and quadrilateral-free**

These properties make it a **candidate for the solution** to our problem.

### Attempt to Add More Edges

Can we construct a graph with **more than 15 edges** on 10 vertices that still avoids 3-cycles and 4-cycles?

- **Average degree** of a graph with 16 edges is 3.2, so at least one vertex must have degree 4.
- Constructing such a graph requires careful placement of edges to **avoid creating 3-cycles or 4-cycles**.
- However, **any attempt to add an edge to the Petersen graph** (which is 3-regular and triangle-free) **creates a triangle**, because of the structure of the graph and the property that any two non-adjacent vertices have **exactly one common neighbor**.
- Constructing a new graph from scratch with 16 or 17 edges and girth at least 5 on 10 vertices is **structurally infeasible**, as all such attempts result in either triangles or quadrilaterals.

### Theoretical Bound

Using a **combinatorial bound** derived from the number of common neighbors and the total number of vertex pairs, we can derive that the **maximum number of edges** $ m $ in such a graph satisfies:

$$
\sum_{v} \binom{d(v)}{2} \leq \binom{10}{2} = 45
$$

This inequality is satisfied by the Petersen graph (with 15 edges), and while it allows for graphs with up to 17 edges, **constructing such graphs without 3-cycles or 4-cycles is not possible**.
