To better analyze their intersection, it's helpful to consider a specific case where the parallelepiped is a unit cube (volume $ V = 1 $). This simplifies calculations while preserving the ratio of volumes due to the invariance of volume ratios under affine transformations.

---

### Finding the Intersection

In the unit cube, the two tetrahedra intersect in a well-known geometric figure: **a regular octahedron**. This octahedron has its six vertices at the centers of the six faces of the cube. These are:

- $ (0.5, 0, 0.5) $
- $ (0.5, 1, 0.5) $
- $ (0, 0.5, 0.5) $
- $ (1, 0.5, 0.5) $
- $ (0.5, 0.5, 0) $
- $ (0.5, 0.5, 1) $

This octahedron is regular and symmetric, and its volume can be calculated as follows:

- The octahedron can be split into two square pyramids, each with a square base of side length $ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} $ and height $ \frac{1}{2} $.
- The base area is $ \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \right)^2 = \frac{1}{2} $, and the height is $ \frac{1}{2} $, so the volume of one pyramid is:
  $$
  \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{12}
  $$
- Since there are two such pyramids, the total volume is:
  $$
  2 \cdot \frac{1}{12} = \frac{1}{6}
  $$

Thus, in the unit cube (where $ V = 1 $), the volume of the intersection of the two tetrahedra is $ \frac{1}{6} $.

---

### General Case

Because the volume of a parallelepiped is invariant under affine transformations (which include skewing and scaling), and the ratio of volumes is preserved, the ratio $ \frac{1}{6} $ of the cube volume holds for any parallelepiped. Therefore, if the parallelepiped has volume $ V $, the volume of the intersection of the two tetrahedra is:

$$
\boxed{\dfrac{V}{6}}
$$
